Lesson 1: Introduction to Cybersecurity

Learning Objectives / Manufofin Koyo

PART A — ENGLISH

1. What is Cybersecurity?

Cybersecurity is the practice of protecting computers, mobile devices, networks, software, online services and digital information from unauthorized access, misuse, damage, disruption or theft.

It combines people, processes and technology. Security is not achieved by installing one application alone; users must also make careful decisions and organizations must follow safe procedures.

Example: Using a strong, unique password, enabling multi-factor authentication, updating your phone, and refusing suspicious links are basic cybersecurity practices.

2. Why is Cybersecurity Important?

  • Privacy: protects personal and confidential information.
  • Financial safety: helps prevent account theft, payment fraud and financial loss.
  • Availability: helps keep important services working.
  • Trust: helps people and organizations use digital services with confidence.
  • Safety: protects systems that support hospitals, schools, businesses and public services.

3. What Does Cybersecurity Protect?

  • Devices: desktop computers, laptops, tablets and smartphones.
  • Networks: Wi-Fi, office networks and internet-connected systems.
  • Applications: mobile apps, websites and business software.
  • Digital information: passwords, photographs, messages, financial records and medical records.
  • Online accounts: email, social media, banking and cloud accounts.

4. Common Basic Security Risks

  • Phishing: a deceptive message or website designed to trick someone into revealing information or opening a harmful link.
  • Malware: harmful software designed to damage, spy on or gain unauthorized access to a device or data.
  • Password attacks: attempts to guess, steal or reuse passwords to enter accounts.
  • Social engineering: manipulating people into revealing information or taking unsafe actions.
  • Data loss: information becoming unavailable, damaged, deleted or stolen.

5. What Does a Cybersecurity Professional Do?

Cybersecurity professionals help identify risks, protect systems, monitor suspicious activity, investigate incidents, improve security controls and teach users safe practices. They must work legally and obtain permission before testing systems.

6. Practical Example

You receive a message claiming that your bank account will be blocked unless you click a link and enter your password. The message creates urgency and asks for secret information. This may be phishing. Do not click the link or enter your password. Contact the bank through its official app, website or verified phone number.

Lesson 2: History of Cybersecurity

Learning Objectives / Manufofin Koyo

PART A — ENGLISH

1. Why Study the History of Cybersecurity?

Cybersecurity did not appear suddenly. It developed as computers became more powerful, connected to one another, and increasingly important to everyday life. Studying its history helps us understand why security controls exist and why attackers and defenders continually adapt.

At first, many computers were large, expensive machines used by a small number of trained people. As computers became smaller and networks expanded, more people could access systems—and the number of possible weaknesses and attacks increased.

2. Early Computing and the Need for Protection

In the early era of computing, security focused heavily on physical protection and controlling who could use a computer. Computers were often kept in restricted rooms. Organizations used procedures, user accounts and access permissions to prevent unauthorized use.

As computers began sharing information through communication networks, protecting the machine itself was no longer enough. Organizations also needed to protect information while it moved between systems.

3. The 1970s and 1980s: Network Research and Early Malware

Research networks connected computers over distance and showed the value—and risk—of connectivity. In 1971, the experimental program known as the Creeper program spread between some systems on ARPANET. It is commonly discussed as an early self-moving experimental program, not as a modern criminal ransomware attack. The Reaper program was created afterward to find and remove Creeper.

During the 1980s, personal computers became more common. The 1986 Brain virus is widely recognized as one of the first viruses for IBM PC-compatible computers. Malware demonstrated that software and removable media could carry threats from one computer to another.

4. The 1990s: The Internet Expands

The public internet and the World Wide Web expanded rapidly. Email, websites and online services created new ways to communicate, but also new ways to spread malicious software, deceive users and attack exposed systems.

Organizations increasingly needed firewalls, antivirus tools, secure configuration, backups and trained staff. Security began to involve whole networks and connected services, not only individual computers.

5. The 2000s: Organized Cybercrime and Wider Attacks

As online banking, shopping and business services grew, criminals increasingly used the internet to steal money, identities and confidential information. Worms, botnets, phishing and large-scale data theft became major concerns.

Security teams developed stronger monitoring, incident response, patch management, encryption and security awareness programs. The goal was not only to prevent attacks, but also to detect them and recover safely.

6. The 2010s to Today: Cloud, Mobile and Advanced Threats

Smartphones, cloud computing, social media, connected devices and remote work have made digital services part of daily life. This has expanded the attack surface: there are more devices, accounts, applications and connections that need protection.

Modern threats include ransomware, credential theft, supply-chain attacks, social engineering and attacks against cloud or internet-connected services. Defenders use multi-factor authentication, encryption, secure software development, backups, threat intelligence and continuous monitoring. Artificial intelligence can support defense, but it can also be misused to make scams more convincing.

7. Important Lesson from History

Technology changes, but several basic security principles remain important: limit access to what people need, keep systems updated, protect sensitive information, make reliable backups, monitor unusual activity, prepare an incident response plan and educate users.

Timeline at a glance
  • Early computing: physical security and controlled access.
  • 1970s: network research and early self-moving programs such as Creeper.
  • 1980s: personal computers and early PC viruses such as Brain.
  • 1990s: wider internet use, email and web-related risks.
  • 2000s: growth of online crime, worms, botnets and phishing.
  • 2010s–today: cloud, mobile, ransomware, connected devices and AI-assisted threats.

Lesson 3: Types of Cyber Threats

Learning Objectives — Manufofin Koyo

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to identify common cyber threats, explain how they work at a high level, recognize warning signs, and describe basic protective actions.

A ƙarshen darasin, za ka iya gane manyan nau'ikan barazanar cyber, bayyana yadda suke aiki a taƙaice, gano alamomin haɗari, da sanin matakan kariya.

1. What Is a Cyber Threat? — Menene Cyber Threat?

A cyber threat is a possible event, action or condition that could harm a computer, phone, network, application or digital information. A threat may try to steal data, disrupt services, damage files or gain unauthorized access.

Cyber threat barazana ce ko wani abu da zai iya cutar da kwamfuta, waya, network, manhaja ko bayanan dijital. Yana iya ƙoƙarin satar bayanai, dakatar da aiki, lalata files ko shiga ba tare da izini ba.

2. Malware — Malicious Software

Malware means software intentionally designed to harm a device, steal information, spy on users or gain unauthorized access. Malware is a broad category; viruses, worms, Trojans, ransomware and spyware are different types.

Malware na nufin manhajar da aka ƙera da niyyar cutar da na'ura, satar bayanai, leƙen asiri ko shiga ba tare da izini ba. Malware babban rukuni ne da ya ƙunshi virus, worm, Trojan, ransomware da spyware.

Computer Virus: Malware that attaches itself to a legitimate file or program and may spread when that infected host is run.
Hausa: Virus yana manne wa wani file ko program; zai iya yaɗuwa idan aka kunna wannan file ko program ɗin da ya kamu.
Worm: Malware that can copy and spread itself between systems, often across networks, without needing to attach to another file.
Hausa: Worm na iya kwafa kansa ya bazu daga na'ura zuwa wata, musamman ta network, ba tare da buƙatar manne wa wani file ba.
Trojan Horse: Malware disguised as a useful or harmless program to trick someone into installing or running it.
Hausa: Trojan malware ne da ke ɓoye kansa kamar manhaja mai amfani domin yaudarar mutum ya girka ko kunna shi.
Ransomware: Malware that locks a device or encrypts files and demands payment. Paying does not guarantee that files will be restored.
Hausa: Ransomware na kulle na'ura ko ɓoye files sannan ya nemi kuɗi. Biyan kuɗi ba ya tabbatar da dawo da files.
Spyware: Software that secretly monitors activity or collects information.
Hausa: Spyware manhaja ce da ke sa ido a ɓoye ko tattara bayanan mutum ba tare da saninsa ba.
Adware: Software that displays unwanted advertisements; some forms may also track users or create security risks.
Hausa: Adware na nuna tallace-tallacen da ba a so; wasu nau'o'insa na iya bibiyar mai amfani ko jawo haɗarin tsaro.

3. Phishing and Social Engineering

Phishing is a deceptive message, email, call or website designed to trick a person into revealing passwords, bank details or other sensitive information, or into opening a harmful link or attachment.

Phishing saƙo, email, kira ko website na yaudara ne da ake amfani da shi domin mutum ya bayar da password, bayanan banki ko wasu sirrika, ko ya buɗe link ko attachment mai haɗari.

Social engineering is the broader manipulation of people to make them reveal information or take an unsafe action. Phishing is one common form of social engineering.

Social engineering ita ce yaudarar mutum ta hanyar amfani da amincewarsa, tsoro ko gaggawa domin ya bayar da bayani ko ya aikata abin da bai kamata ba. Phishing na ɗaya daga cikin hanyoyinta.

4. Other Important Threats — Wasu Muhimman Barazana

  • DoS / DDoS: An attempt to make a service unavailable by overwhelming it with traffic. DDoS uses many sources. Ƙoƙarin cika sabis da yawan buƙatu har ya kasa amsa wa masu amfani; DDoS yana amfani da na'urori ko wurare da yawa.
  • Password attacks: Attempts to guess, steal or misuse passwords. Ƙoƙarin zato, satar password ko amfani da password da aka samu ba bisa izini ba.
  • Insider threat: Risk caused by someone with authorized access who misuses it, deliberately or accidentally. Haɗarin da ya taso daga mutum mai izinin shiga tsarin wanda ya yi amfani da izinin ba daidai ba, da gangan ko bisa kuskure.
  • Zero-day vulnerability: A software weakness that is exploited before an effective fix is available to the affected users. Raunin software da ake amfani da shi kafin masu amfani su samu ingantaccen gyara.

5. Warning Signs — Alamomin Gargadi

  • Unexpected attachments or links, especially with pressure to act quickly.
  • Requests for passwords, one-time codes or bank details.
  • Unexpected pop-ups, unknown apps or unusual device behavior.
  • Files suddenly renamed, inaccessible or showing ransom messages.
  • Accounts sending messages you did not write or login alerts you do not recognize.

Ka kula da links ko attachments da ba ka zata ba, saƙonnin da ke matsa maka ka yi sauri, neman password ko OTP, apps da ba ka sani ba, files da suka kulle, da sanarwar shiga account da ba kai ka yi ba.

6. How to Reduce the Risk — Yadda Ake Rage Haɗari

  1. Keep your operating system, browser and apps updated.
  2. Use long, unique passwords and a password manager where possible.
  3. Turn on multi-factor authentication (MFA).
  4. Do not open suspicious links or attachments; verify through a trusted official channel.
  5. Install software only from trusted sources.

PART A — ENGLISH EXPLANATION

1. What Is a Computer?

A computer is an electronic device that accepts data, processes it according to instructions, produces useful output, and can store the results for later use. Computers may be desktop PCs, laptops, tablets, smartphones, servers or embedded devices inside machines.

Example: When you type marks into a spreadsheet, the computer accepts the numbers, calculates the total or average, displays the result and can save the file.

2. The Basic Computer Cycle: Input, Processing, Output and Storage

INPUT → PROCESSING → OUTPUT
↘ STORAGE stores data and results for later use
Input: Data or instructions entered into a computer, for example typing with a keyboard, touching a screen, scanning a document or speaking into a microphone.
Processing: The computer works on the input by following instructions. The CPU performs many of the main processing operations.
Output: The result produced, such as text on a monitor, printed pages, sound from speakers or a displayed calculation.
Storage: Saving data and programs so they can be used later, for example on an SSD, hard disk, USB flash drive or memory card.

Important: Storage is not the same as memory. RAM temporarily holds data and instructions currently in use; a storage drive keeps files when the computer is switched off.

3. Main Parts of a Computer System

  • Hardware: The physical parts that can be touched, such as the motherboard, CPU, RAM, keyboard, screen and storage drive.
  • Software: Programs and instructions that tell hardware what to do, including operating systems and applications.
  • Data: Raw facts entered or stored, such as names, numbers, images and measurements.
  • Users: People who operate the computer and use its results.
  • Procedures: Agreed steps for using and managing computer systems safely.

4. The Central Processing Unit (CPU)

The CPU is often called the brain of the computer because it executes instructions and coordinates many processing activities. Its performance is affected by several factors, including its design, number of cores, clock speed, cooling and the workload.

Do not confuse: The CPU is not the whole computer. A computer also needs memory, storage, a motherboard, power and input/output devices to operate as a complete system.

5. Memory: RAM and ROM

RAM — Random Access Memory: Fast, temporary working memory used by programs and the operating system while the computer is running. Its contents are normally lost when power is removed.
ROM — Read-Only Memory: A general name for non-volatile memory used to store firmware or instructions that should remain available without normal power. Modern systems may use rewritable flash memory for firmware.

More RAM can help a computer handle more active programs, but RAM does not replace long-term storage such as an SSD or hard disk.

6. Data and Information

Data means raw facts that may not yet have meaning on their own. Information is data that has been processed or organized so that it is useful.

Example: The numbers 45, 60 and 75 are data. Their calculated average, 60, is information that helps someone understand the results.

7. Types of Computers

  • Desktop computer: Usually designed to stay in one place, such as an office workstation.
  • Laptop: A portable computer with a built-in screen, keyboard and battery.
  • Tablet and smartphone: Portable touchscreen computers used for communication, applications and internet services.
  • Server: A computer that provides services or resources to other computers over a network.
  • Embedded computer: A computer built into another device, such as a vehicle, router or medical machine.

8. Why Computer Fundamentals Matter in Cybersecurity

Cybersecurity professionals need to understand what they are protecting. Knowing the difference between RAM and storage helps when investigating data loss. Understanding hardware and software helps identify where a fault or weakness may exist. Understanding users and procedures helps reduce mistakes such as sharing passwords or installing unsafe programs.

Computer fundamentals also help you understand later topics such as operating systems, networking, access control, malware and data protection.

Lesson 6: Operating Systems

Learning Objectives / Manufofin Koyo

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to define an operating system, explain its main functions, identify common operating systems, understand user accounts and permissions, and describe basic ways to keep an operating system secure.

A ƙarshen wannan darasi, za ka iya bayyana ma'anar Operating System, ayyukansa, gane nau'o'in operating systems, fahimtar user accounts da permissions, da matakan kare operating system.

PART A — ENGLISH

1. What is an Operating System?

An Operating System (OS) is the main system software that manages a computer's hardware and software resources and provides a platform for applications to run. It acts as a bridge between the user, applications and computer hardware.

Examples: Microsoft Windows, Linux, macOS, Android and iOS.

2. Main Functions of an Operating System

  • Process management: manages running programs and shares CPU time between them.
  • Memory management: allocates and releases RAM for programs that need it.
  • File management: organizes files and folders and controls how they are stored and accessed.
  • Device management: coordinates hardware such as keyboards, printers, storage devices and network adapters, often with the help of drivers.
  • User interface: provides a way to interact with the computer, such as a Graphical User Interface (GUI) or Command-Line Interface (CLI).
  • Security and permissions: manages user accounts, sign-in and access rights.

3. Types and Examples of Operating Systems

  • Desktop operating systems: Windows, macOS and desktop Linux distributions.
  • Mobile operating systems: Android and iOS.
  • Server operating systems: Windows Server and Linux server distributions, designed to provide services to other computers.
  • Embedded operating systems: software in dedicated devices such as routers, smart televisions and some industrial equipment.

4. GUI and CLI

A Graphical User Interface (GUI) lets users interact through windows, icons, menus and a pointer or touch screen. A Command-Line Interface (CLI) lets users enter text commands. Both are ways of interacting with an operating system. Commands can make powerful changes, so users should understand a command before running it.

5. User Accounts, Authentication and Permissions

An operating system can have multiple user accounts. A standard user normally has limited permissions for everyday work. An administrator account can make system-wide changes, install software and manage other accounts. Use administrator privileges only when necessary; do not share administrator passwords.

Authentication checks who is signing in. Authorization determines what that authenticated user is allowed to do. A strong password and multi-factor authentication, where available, help protect accounts.

6. Operating System Updates and Security

  • Install security updates from the official operating system update service.
  • Use a supported operating system; versions that no longer receive security updates become increasingly risky.
  • Enable a firewall and use reputable security protection where appropriate.
  • Install applications from trusted sources and remove software you no longer need.
  • Use a standard account for everyday activities and restrict administrator access.
  • Back up important files regularly and test that you can restore them.
  • Lock your device when you leave it unattended.

7. Why Operating Systems Matter in Cybersecurity

The operating system controls access to files, accounts, devices, memory and network connections. If it is outdated, misconfigured or compromised by malware, attackers may steal information, install unwanted programs or disrupt services. Keeping the OS updated, limiting permissions and monitoring unusual activity reduce these risks.

Important Safety Rule

Practice system administration only on devices you own or are authorized to manage. Do not disable security controls or run commands you do not understand on a real device.

Lesson 7: Internet Fundamentals

Learning Objectives / Manufofin Koyo

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to explain what the Internet is, distinguish the Internet from the World Wide Web, describe how devices access online services, identify common Internet terms, understand the basic journey of a web request, and recognize safe browsing practices.

PART A — ENGLISH

1. What is the Internet?

The Internet is a worldwide system of interconnected computer networks. These networks use agreed communication rules called protocols to exchange data. The Internet connects billions of devices, including computers, phones, servers and smart devices.

The Internet is infrastructure that carries data. It supports many services, such as the World Wide Web, email, online messaging, voice and video calls, file transfers, online banking and cloud services.

Your device → Wi-Fi router or mobile network → Internet Service Provider (ISP) → Internet networks → Online server or service

2. Internet vs World Wide Web

The Internet is the global network infrastructure. The World Wide Web (WWW or Web) is one service that operates over the Internet. Websites and web pages are accessed using a web browser, usually through HTTP or HTTPS.

Example: Sending an email or making an Internet voice call uses the Internet, but it is not the same thing as browsing a website. The Web is part of what the Internet makes possible; they are not identical.

3. How Do People Connect to the Internet?

  • ISP (Internet Service Provider): a company or organization that provides Internet access, such as through fixed broadband or mobile data.
  • Router: forwards data between your local network and other networks. A home Wi-Fi router commonly lets several devices share one Internet connection.
  • Modem or access equipment: connects a home or organization to the provider's access network. The exact equipment depends on the connection type.
  • Wi-Fi: a wireless networking technology that connects devices to a local network. Wi-Fi itself is not the Internet; the router or access point may connect that local network to the Internet.
  • Mobile data: Internet access provided through a mobile network and a SIM/eSIM-enabled device.
  • Ethernet: a wired network connection, often used for stable connections between a device and a router or switch.

4. Client, Server and Data

A client is a device or application that requests a service. A server is a computer or software system that responds to requests and provides data or services.

Example: When you open a news website, your browser (the client) sends a request. The website's server responds with information that the browser displays. The server may also communicate with other systems, such as a database.

5. Browser, Search Engine, Website and URL

  • Web browser: software used to open and interact with websites, such as Chrome, Firefox, Edge or Safari.
  • Search engine: an online service that helps users find information on the Web, such as Google or Bing. A search engine is not the same as a browser.
  • Website: a collection of related web pages and resources under a domain or web address.
  • Web page: one individual page or document displayed in a browser.
  • URL (Uniform Resource Locator): the address used to locate a resource on the Web. It can include a scheme (such as https), a domain name and a path.
  • Domain name: a human-readable name, such as example.com, that can be translated into an IP address using DNS.

Example URL: https://www.example.com/news. Here, https is the scheme, www.example.com is the host name, and /news is the path.

6. What Happens When You Open a Website?

  1. You enter a website address or select a link in your browser.
  2. The device needs to find the network address for the website. The Domain Name System (DNS) can translate the domain name into an IP address.
  3. Your device sends data through its local network and Internet provider toward the destination server. Routers help forward the data across networks.
  4. The browser and server communicate using an appropriate protocol, commonly HTTPS for secure websites.
  5. The server sends a response. Your browser processes the response and displays the page.

This is a simplified explanation. Real connections may involve caches, content delivery networks, encryption handshakes, proxies and several servers.

7. Data Packets and Protocols

Information sent across networks is commonly divided into smaller units called packets. Network equipment forwards these packets toward their destination, where the data is reassembled or processed. Protocols are agreed rules that allow devices to communicate.

  • IP (Internet Protocol): helps address and route data between networks.
  • TCP: provides reliable, ordered delivery for applications that use it.
  • UDP: provides a simpler transport method often used when low delay is important and the application handles reliability as needed.
  • HTTP: a protocol used to transfer web resources.
  • HTTPS: HTTP protected by TLS encryption, helping protect data in transit and authenticate the website.

We will study networking and these protocols in more detail in later roadmap topics. For now, remember that the Internet depends on connected networks, addressing and communication rules.

8. Internet Services

  • Web: websites, web applications and online portals.
  • Email: sending and receiving electronic messages.
  • Messaging and social media: exchanging text, images, video and other content.
  • Voice and video calls: real-time communication over Internet connections.
  • Cloud services: storing files or using computing resources hosted on remote systems.
  • Online transactions: banking, shopping, payments and booking services.

9. Basic Internet Safety

  • Check the website address carefully; look for misspellings and misleading domain names.
  • Use HTTPS websites for sensitive activities, but remember that HTTPS alone does not prove a website is honest or safe.
  • Never enter passwords, PINs or one-time codes after following an unexpected message link. Open the official app or type the known address yourself.
  • Use strong, unique passwords and multi-factor authentication where available.
  • Avoid sensitive transactions on public or untrusted Wi-Fi when possible. If you must use it, use trusted apps and verify the connection.
  • Keep your browser, operating system and applications updated.
  • Download files and applications only from trusted sources; be cautious with unexpected attachments and pop-ups.
  • Do not assume information is true just because it appears online. Check important claims against reliable sources.
Cybersecurity Reminder

A padlock icon or HTTPS means the connection is encrypted and the certificate is accepted by the browser. It does not guarantee that the website itself is legitimate, truthful or free from scams.

1. What is a Computer Network? / Menene Computer Network?

A computer network is a group of two or more computers or digital devices connected so they can communicate and share resources such as files, printers, applications and Internet access.

Computer Network haɗin kwamfutoci ko na'urorin zamani biyu ko fiye ne domin su iya sadarwa da musayar abubuwa kamar files, printer, applications da Internet.

Example: Computers in a school laboratory connected to one printer form a network.

2. Why Do We Use Networks? / Me ya sa ake amfani da Network?

  • Communication: devices exchange information. / Na'urori suna musayar bayanai.
  • Resource sharing: share printers, files and storage. / Raba printer, files da ma'ajiyar bayanai.
  • Internet sharing: many devices can use one Internet connection. / Na'urori da yawa su yi amfani da haɗin Internet guda.
  • Central management: an administrator can manage users and devices. / Mai kula da tsarin zai iya sarrafa users da na'urori.

3. Types of Networks / Nau'o'in Network

PAN — Personal Area Network: a very small network around one person, such as a phone connected to wireless earbuds by Bluetooth.

PAN network ne ƙarami kusa da mutum, misali waya da aka haɗa da wireless earbuds ta Bluetooth.

LAN — Local Area Network: connects devices in a limited area such as a home, office, school or laboratory.

LAN yana haɗa na'urori a wuri kaɗan kamar gida, ofis, makaranta ko dakin gwaje-gwaje.

WLAN — Wireless Local Area Network: a LAN that uses Wi-Fi instead of network cables.

WLAN shi ne LAN da ke amfani da Wi-Fi maimakon wayoyin network.

MAN — Metropolitan Area Network: connects networks across a town or city.

MAN yana haɗa networks a faɗin gari ko birni.

WAN — Wide Area Network: connects networks over large geographical areas, sometimes across countries. The Internet is the best-known example of a global network of networks.

WAN yana haɗa networks a manyan wurare, har ma tsakanin ƙasashe. Internet shi ne sanannen misalin network mai faɗi sosai.

4. Basic Network Devices / Muhimman Na'urorin Network

  • Switch: connects multiple wired devices inside a LAN and forwards data to the appropriate device. / Yana haɗa na'urori da yawa ta cable a LAN, sannan ya aika bayanai ga na'urar da ta dace.
  • Access Point (AP): allows wireless devices to join a network using Wi-Fi. / Yana ba na'urorin wireless damar shiga network ta Wi-Fi.
  • Modem: connects a network to the service provider's communication line; the exact role depends on the Internet technology. / Yana haɗa network da layin sadarwar kamfanin Internet; aikinsa ya danganta da fasahar da ake amfani da ita.
  • Network cable (Ethernet): carries data between wired network devices. / Kebul ne da ke ɗaukar bayanai tsakanin na'urorin network.

5. Wired and Wireless Networks / Network Mai Cable da Mara Cable

Wired: uses physical cables, commonly Ethernet. It can provide stable speed and is often preferred for fixed computers and servers.

Wired yana amfani da cable. Yana iya ba da haɗi mai ƙarfi da sauri, musamman ga desktop da servers.

Wireless: uses radio signals such as Wi-Fi. It offers mobility and convenience, but signal strength, distance and interference can affect performance.
